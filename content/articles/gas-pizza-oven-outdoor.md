Getting pizza right outdoors is mostly a heat problem. Standard grills don't get hot enough, and most backyards don't have room for a wood-fired brick oven. A dedicated [gas pizza oven for outdoor use](/pizza-ovens/) solves both issues , high temperatures, fast cook times, and enough portability that you can move it when the HOA starts asking questions.

The catch is that not all outdoor pizza ovens are the same, and the differences matter more than the marketing suggests. Fuel type, stone size, and peak temperature all determine whether you get a genuinely charred crust or an expensive flatbread. Here's what I found after going through these five models.

## What to Look For in an Outdoor Gas Pizza Oven

### Peak Temperature and How Fast It Gets There

Peak temperature is the single most important spec on any outdoor pizza oven. Neapolitan-style pizza requires 800°F to 950°F , that's where you get the leopard spotting on the crust and a cook time measured in seconds rather than minutes. Most home ovens cap out around 550°F, which is why your homemade pizza always tastes like something's missing.

Getting to temperature fast matters too. An oven that takes forty-five minutes to reach 700°F is one you'll use less often. Look for models that reach working temperature in fifteen to twenty minutes , that's the realistic threshold for a weeknight cook after the kids' soccer ends.

### Stone Material and Size

The cooking stone is where your pizza actually lives, and the material is not interchangeable. Cordierite stone is the standard worth seeking out , it handles thermal shock better than cheap ceramic, holds heat more evenly, and doesn't crack when a cold dough ball hits a 900°F surface. A stone that cracks after three uses is not a deal, regardless of how attractive the initial price looks.

Size matters in a practical sense. A 12-inch stone limits you to personal-size pizzas. That's fine for one or two people, but if you're feeding a group, you're running multiple cooks and managing the crowd between rounds. A 15-inch stone lets you run a 14-inch pie, which is closer to what most families actually want.

### Fuel Flexibility vs. Simplicity

Gas and propane ovens are convenient. You dial in temperature, you hit ignite, and you cook. There's no babysitting a wood fire, no sourcing hardwood, and no smoke complaints from neighbors already annoyed about the Traeger. For a suburban backyard , or a patio with HOA covenants , gas is genuinely the right call.

Multi-fuel ovens add flexibility, but flexibility comes with trade-offs. An oven optimized for wood, gas, and electric simultaneously is often less than optimal in any one mode. If you know you're cooking on gas ninety percent of the time, a dedicated gas unit will perform more consistently than a multi-fuel oven with a gas adapter bolted on. Explore the full range of [pizza ovens](/pizza-ovens/) before committing to a fuel configuration , the right choice depends on how you actually cook, not how you imagine you might cook someday.

### Portability and Setup Reality

Every portable pizza oven in this category claims to be easy to move. Most of them are, with the caveat that "portable" and "quick setup" are not the same thing. An oven that requires assembling multiple components, attaching a burner separately, and leveling the stone every time is portable in the sense that you can put it in a truck , not portable in the sense that you'd take it camping.

Think honestly about where you're cooking. If the answer is "my patio, always," portability is a nice-to-have and shouldn't drive your decision. If you're cooking at tailgates, campsites, or a family member's place on holidays, weight and setup time become genuine factors.

## Buying Guide

### Temperature Is the Deciding Variable

Before anything else, decide what cook temperature you actually need. If you want authentic Neapolitan pizza , thin crust, significant char, sixty-second cook , you need an oven that reaches 900°F or above. The Ooni Karu 12 and the BIG HORN 12" Multi-Fuel both get there. If you're making New York-style or thicker-crust pizza, 700°F to 800°F produces excellent results without the tighter timing that high-temperature cooking demands.

Matching temperature to your cooking style saves money and prevents disappointment. Buying a 950°F-capable oven for thick-crust pizza is overkill. Buying a 700°F oven and expecting Neapolitan results is frustration.

### Stone Size vs. Cooking Capacity

Twelve-inch stone ovens dominate this category because they're portable and efficient to heat. They're also limiting if you're feeding more than two people. A 13-inch or 15-inch stone lets you run a full-size personal or family pizza without the same rotation bottleneck.

Think about your typical cook. Two people eating two small pizzas each? A 12-inch oven works. Four people expecting dinner in reasonable sequence? The larger stone earns its footprint. This is the choice that most buyers underestimate until they're running their third consecutive cook while people wait.

### Fuel Simplicity vs. Multi-Fuel Flexibility

Gas and propane are the right choice for most suburban backyards. They're clean, controllable, and HOA-friendly. Multi-fuel ovens are appealing in concept , wood for weekends, gas for weeknights , but that flexibility costs money and sometimes costs consistency. Not every multi-fuel oven performs equally well in every mode.

If wood-fired flavor is genuinely important to you, choose an oven designed primarily for wood. If convenience is the priority, choose a dedicated gas unit. Reading through the [pizza oven](/pizza-ovens/) options organized by fuel type before deciding is worth the time , seeing the full category side by side clarifies what you're trading.

### Portability: Real vs. Marketing

Every oven in this roundup is technically portable. Not all of them are practically portable. Weight, setup steps, and whether the burner is included all determine whether you'll actually move the oven or whether it'll live permanently on your patio.

If you're planning to cook at locations other than your home , tailgates, campsites, a parent's house during the holidays , weigh setup time as seriously as weight. An oven that requires ten minutes of configuration every time you use it is not a camping oven. An oven that connects to a standard propane tank and lights in sixty seconds is.

### Accessory Dependencies and Total Cost

The BIG HORN 12" Multi-Fuel lists a high-performance ceiling, but the burner is sold separately. That's not a flaw , it's a system design choice that gives you flexibility in fuel configuration. It does, however, mean the listed product price understates what you'll spend to cook your first pizza.

Before choosing any oven, confirm what's included. Stone included? Burner included? Gas adapter, cover, and peel are often sold separately across all brands. The difference between an oven that's ready to cook and one that requires three accessory orders is worth knowing before you commit.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What temperature does an outdoor gas pizza oven need to reach to make good pizza?

For Neapolitan-style pizza with a charred, blistered crust, you need a cooking surface temperature of at least 800°F, with 900°F to 950°F being the target range. New York-style or thicker-crust pizzas cook well in the 650°F to 750°F range with longer cook times. The [Ooni Karu 12](product:ooni-karu-12-multi-fuel) reaches 950°F and is the most capable option in this group for high-temperature Neapolitan cooking.
### Is the Ooni Karu 12 or the Solo Stove Pi Prime the better choice for a beginner?

The [Solo Stove Pi Prime](product:solo-stove-pi-prime-pizza) is the easier starting point for most beginners. It runs on gas exclusively, which means no wood sourcing, no fire management, and consistent temperature control from the first cook. The Ooni Karu 12 offers higher peak temperatures and multi-fuel flexibility, but the wood-fire mode adds complexity that beginners don't need on their first few cooks.
### Do I need to buy a pizza peel separately, or does it come with these ovens?

None of the ovens in this roundup include a pizza peel. A peel is essential , you cannot safely launch and retrieve pizza from a 900°F oven without one. Budget for a peel alongside your oven purchase, and choose one with a handle long enough that your forearm stays out of the opening during the cook.
### Can I use these gas pizza ovens in a covered outdoor space?

Gas and propane ovens produce carbon monoxide and require adequate ventilation. A fully enclosed space is not safe. A covered patio or pergola with open sides and good airflow is generally workable, but position the oven away from walls and ensure exhaust can escape. When in doubt, cook in the open air , these ovens are designed for outdoor use.
### What is cordierite stone, and does it matter which stone my oven uses?

Cordierite is a mineral used in high-temperature cooking surfaces because it handles thermal shock , the rapid heating and cooling that pizza cooking involves , without cracking. It also distributes heat evenly, which prevents hot spots that burn the crust in one section while undercooking another. The [Solo Stove Pi Prime](product:solo-stove-pi-prime-pizza) uses cordierite, which is a meaningful spec, not marketing language.
