Flat top cooking is one of those things that looks simple until you're standing at the griddle managing four different foods at once, each one wanting a different temperature. A good Blackstone griddle makes that manageable , a bad setup turns breakfast for six into a logistical problem. I've spent enough time reading about [griddles](/griddles/) and cooking on them to know that the spec sheet rarely tells the whole story.

The products in this category vary more than you'd expect. Cooking surface size, burner count, hood design, and grease management all shape the experience in ways that matter on Saturday morning.

## What to Look For in Blackstone Grills and Griddles

### Cooking Surface Size

Surface area determines what's possible at any given moment. A 28-inch griddle handles a family breakfast or a weeknight dinner without crowding. Step up to 36 inches and you're cooking for a party , smash burgers, eggs, and toast running simultaneously without any of it getting cold while the rest finishes.

The practical catch is footprint. A 36-inch griddle is a large piece of equipment that needs real estate on your patio. If your outdoor space is limited , a concrete pad, a small deck, a setup that has to share space with chairs and kids , the 28-inch option often makes more sense than its bigger sibling. Cooking surface size is the first variable to settle before anything else.

### Burner Count and Heat Zone Control

Two burners and three burners sound similar. In practice they're not. A two-burner setup gives you high and low zones, which covers most cooking scenarios well. A three-burner layout adds a meaningful third zone , useful when you're running a protein on high heat, holding cooked items warm on low, and keeping a middle zone at medium for eggs or vegetables.

Independent burner control is what separates griddle cooking from conventional grill cooking. You're not flipping things to manage doneness , you're moving food laterally across a temperature gradient. More burners means a finer gradient. That matters more the more ambitious your cook gets.

### Hood and Weather Protection

A hood does two things that matter. It traps heat above the cooking surface, which helps with thicker proteins that need ambient heat to finish without burning the bottom. It also protects the griddle surface when the unit isn't in use , grease, debris, and moisture are the primary enemies of a well-seasoned flat top.

Not every Blackstone ships with a hood as standard. On hooded models, check whether the hood is integrated or an add-on, and whether it sits close enough to the cooking surface to actually retain heat effectively. A hood that opens wide and sits high is mostly decorative. One that seals down reasonably close does real cooking work.

### Grease Management System

Flat top cooking produces grease in volume. Without a well-designed drainage system, that grease pools, smokes, and eventually becomes a fire risk. Rear grease management systems channel runoff away from the cooking surface toward a rear collection point rather than the front of the unit, which keeps the grease out of the path of the cook.

Front-drain systems work, but they require more active management , the drainage cup sits right where you're standing. Rear systems are tidier. If you're cooking bacon, sausage, or anything else with significant fat content regularly, the grease management design is worth evaluating carefully before you commit to a unit. Checking the broader [flat top griddle](/griddles/) landscape confirms this is one of the most commonly cited maintenance factors.

### Portability vs. Permanence

Caster wheels sound like a minor convenience. For patio cooking in a space that gets used for multiple purposes, the ability to roll the griddle into position and back against the house wall matters. Some Blackstone models are genuinely portable , lighter, folding-leg designs meant for camping or tailgating. Others use wheels to mean "movable around a patio" rather than "packable into a truck."

Know which category you're buying. A unit with caster wheels and extendable side shelves is convenient for patio use but isn't the same as a camping griddle. Match the design intent to how you actually plan to use it.

## Buying Guide

### How Much Surface Do You Actually Need?

The temptation is to buy the largest cooking surface available. The reality is that more surface means more to maintain, more to season, and more weight to deal with if you move the unit at all. A 28-inch griddle feeds a family of four with room to work. A 36-inch surface starts making sense at six or more people, or when you're cooking for guests regularly.

Surface area also affects how long the griddle takes to heat evenly. A smaller top reaches consistent temperature faster, which matters on weeknight cooks where you don't want to spend ten minutes preheating. Match the size to your typical cook , not your largest imagined occasion.

### Burner Configuration and Temperature Zones

Two burners handle most residential cooking scenarios without issue. Where three-burner layouts earn their keep is in the ability to create a true intermediate zone , something between full blast and warm-holding that a two-burner setup can't replicate cleanly without improvisation.

If your typical cook involves a single protein and a side, two burners is sufficient. If you're regularly running three or more different foods at different stages, the third burner pays for itself in reduced stress. This is worth thinking through before you buy rather than after. The full range of [griddle configurations](/griddles/) spans both, and knowing your use case makes the decision straightforward.

### Hood: Useful Tool or Added Bulk?

A hood on a residential griddle does two useful things: it traps heat for better convective cooking on thicker proteins, and it protects the seasoned surface from debris and moisture when the griddle is idle. Both matter. The question is whether the hood on a given model actually accomplishes both or whether it's primarily aesthetic.

Integrated hoods that sit close to the cooking surface and close securely are the ones that work. Hoods that open very wide and don't seal tightly are mostly useful for keeping light rain off the surface when you're mid-cook. If a hood is important to you, check the design specifics before assuming all hoods perform similarly.

### Maintenance Reality for Flat Top Griddles

Flat top griddles require more consistent maintenance than conventional grill grates. The cooking surface needs to be seasoned before first use and re-seasoned periodically , thin layers of oil baked onto the steel build up the non-stick patina that makes flat top cooking work properly. Neglecting that step is how you end up with food sticking and rust forming at the edges.

The grease management system design matters here too. Units with rear drainage keep the cleanup process simpler, because grease channels away consistently rather than pooling at the front where it can carbonize unevenly. Budget ten minutes of post-cook maintenance , scrape the surface while it's still warm, wipe it down, add a light oil coat , and the griddle stays in good shape indefinitely.

### Accessories: What You Need From Day One

A bare griddle is incomplete. At minimum, you need a good flat-edged spatula (or two), a bench scraper for surface cleanup, and a basting cover for finishing cooks. These aren't optional extras , they're functional requirements for cooking on a flat top correctly.

Buying an accessory kit designed for Blackstone surfaces specifically is more practical than assembling a set piecemeal. Generic grill tools often have the wrong edge geometry for a flat top surface. Purpose-built flat top spatulas are thinner, wider, and more maneuverable than conventional grill spatulas, which makes a meaningful difference when you're moving food around a 28-inch surface under time pressure.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the main difference between the 28-inch and 36-inch Blackstone griddles?

The 36-inch model offers significantly more cooking surface, which matters when you're feeding large groups or running multiple foods simultaneously at scale. The 28-inch models are more practical for most residential patios , smaller footprint, faster preheat, easier to maintain. Unless you regularly cook for six or more people, the 28-inch surface handles the job without the added bulk of the larger unit.
### Do Blackstone griddles work better with two burners or three?

Three burners give you finer heat zone control, which makes a real difference when you're managing multiple foods at different temperatures. The [28 Inch Blackstone Griddle 2080](product:28-inch-blackstone-griddle-2080) three-burner layout on a 28-inch surface is particularly well-suited for this. Two burners work well for simpler cooks , a hot zone and a warm zone covers most everyday scenarios without the added complexity.
### Do I need a hood on a Blackstone griddle?

A hood earns its place if you cook thicker proteins that benefit from trapped ambient heat, or if your griddle lives outdoors and needs surface protection between uses. It's less essential if you cook primarily thinner foods like eggs, smash burgers, and vegetables that finish quickly on direct heat. The [BLACKSTONE 1883 Original 28" Griddle](product:blackstone-1883-original-28-griddle) integrates the hood well, making it a genuinely functional addition rather than just a cosmetic feature.
### How often does a Blackstone griddle surface need to be seasoned?

Season the surface before first use, and plan to add a light oil coat after every cook. A deeper re-seasoning , multiple thin layers of oil baked on , is worth doing if you notice the surface starting to stick or rust appearing at the edges. In practice, regular post-cook oiling prevents most problems.
