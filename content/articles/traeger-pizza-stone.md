Getting good pizza off a Traeger isn't magic , it's stone management. The right [pizza stone](/pizza-ovens/) absorbs heat evenly, pulls moisture from the dough, and gives you that char on the bottom that a pan will never produce. What separates a useful stone from a frustrating one is material, mass, and how well it handles the temperature swings that pellet grills deliver.

Most buyers find out too late that size and shape matter as much as the stone itself. A stone that fits your indoor oven perfectly may sit awkwardly on a Traeger grill grate, blocking airflow or leaving no room to rotate. These five options cover the realistic range of what works.

## What to Look For in a Pizza Stone for Your Traeger

### Material and Heat Absorption

Cordierite is the standard for serious pizza stones, and for good reason. It's a naturally porous mineral that absorbs moisture from the dough base while conducting heat steadily from below. That combination is what creates a crisp bottom crust without burning the top. The porosity also means cordierite handles rapid temperature changes better than cheaper ceramic alternatives , a meaningful advantage when you're placing a cold or room-temperature stone on a hot grill grate, or pulling a stone from the cold outdoors into a preheated cooker.

Avoid glazed stones for Traeger use. A glaze seals the surface and defeats the moisture-wicking purpose entirely. You want raw, unglazed cordierite that can do the work.

### Size and Grill Fit

A Traeger Pro 575 has a main grate area of roughly 575 square inches. That sounds generous, but a pizza stone should never cover the entire grate , you need airflow around the edges to maintain convection and allow smoke circulation. A stone in the 15 × 12 inch rectangular range, or a 16-inch round, hits the practical sweet spot for most Traeger owners. It's large enough to cook a full pizza without being so large it chokes the cook chamber.

Measure your specific grate before ordering. Grates on the Junior, Pro, Ironwood, and Timberline series differ meaningfully. A stone that works on one model may be too tight on another.

### Thickness and Thermal Mass

Thicker stones hold more heat and recover faster between launches. This matters most when you're cooking multiple pizzas in sequence , a thin stone will cool significantly after the first pie comes off, and your second pizza will suffer for it. Look for stones at least half an inch thick; three-quarters of an inch is better if you're cooking for a crowd.

More mass also means longer preheat time. Budget at least 45 minutes at your target temperature before the first pizza goes on. Rushing preheat is the most common reason a stone produces a pale, doughy bottom crust.

### Accessories That Actually Help

A wooden peel is useful for launching raw dough. An aluminum peel , especially a long-handled one , is better for retrieving a cooked pizza from a hot Traeger, where the distance between your hand and the grate matters. If a stone set includes both, or includes a long-handled aluminum peel, that's a practical combination worth factoring in.

A pizza cutter included in a bundle rarely justifies the bundle on its own, but if you're building out a pizza setup from scratch, having one in the box saves a separate purchase.

Exploring the broader world of [outdoor pizza cooking equipment](/pizza-ovens/) before committing to a single stone is worth the time , particularly if you're deciding between a dedicated pizza oven and a stone-on-grill approach.

## Buying Guide

### Stone Shape: Rectangular vs. Round

Rectangular stones give you more usable surface area per square inch of grate space, and they're more versatile if you cook anything beyond pizza , bread, calzones, flatbreads, and sheet-style items all benefit from corners. Round stones are optimized for pizza specifically and nothing else. On a Traeger, both shapes work; the question is whether you're building a pizza-specific setup or a general baking surface for the grill.

For most Traeger owners, rectangular is the practical default. It fits the grill grate geometry more efficiently and gives you more flexibility on subsequent cooks.

### Choosing Between a Stone and a Dedicated Pizza Oven

A pizza stone on a Traeger is a capable workaround, not a replacement for a dedicated high-temperature pizza oven. Traegers typically cap out around 500°F, while purpose-built [pizza ovens](/pizza-ovens/) reach 700, 900°F , the temperature range where Neapolitan-style leopard spotting and 90-second cook times happen. If your goal is that style of pizza, a stone on a pellet grill is a stepping stone, not the destination.

If your goal is a solid, smoke-kissed pizza on a cooker you already own, a quality stone on a Traeger delivers that reliably and without additional equipment investment.

### Preheat Time and Temperature Management

The most consistent mistake with pizza stones on a Traeger is insufficient preheat. The stone needs time to saturate with heat all the way through its mass , surface temperature and core temperature are different things, and a thermometer on the grill dome doesn't tell you what the stone is actually holding. Forty-five minutes at your target temperature is the reliable floor. An infrared thermometer pointed directly at the stone surface is the most useful tool you can add to this setup.

Run the Traeger with the lid closed during preheat. Opening the lid bleeds heat and extends the time before the stone is ready.

### Single Stone vs. Multi-Stone Sets

Multi-stone sets create the option to run two pizzas simultaneously on different grate levels, which matters if you're cooking for more than four people regularly. The practical limitation is that Traeger convection is strongest at the main grate; an upper rack stone will cook differently , usually slower on the bottom , than the primary position. Calibrate your expectations before a dinner party.

A single, high-quality stone at the main grate level will outperform two mediocre stones stacked in a setup your grill wasn't optimized for.

### Cleaning and Longevity

Never use soap on a pizza stone. The porous cordierite will absorb it and transfer flavor to your next cook. Scrape residue with a stiff brush or plastic scraper once the stone is completely cool , stone temperature and water temperature should always be close to matching before any moisture contact. Discoloration and dark spots are normal; they don't indicate a problem and don't require remediation.

Store the stone somewhere it won't be exposed to sudden moisture. A stone that has absorbed water and then gets placed on a 500°F grate is at real risk of cracking. A dry storage location and a slow initial preheat protect against the most common cause of stone failure.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I use any pizza stone on a Traeger, or do I need a grill-specific one?

Any unglazed cordierite stone rated for high heat can work on a Traeger. The key requirements are that the stone is unglazed, large enough to be useful without covering the entire grate, and thick enough to hold heat through a cook. Stones marketed as "oven only" are usually fine on a grill as well , the distinction is a marketing label, not a material difference, as long as the stone is cordierite and the temperature range falls within Traeger's operating window.
### What size pizza stone fits a Traeger Pro 575?

A 15 × 12 inch rectangular stone or a 15, 16 inch round stone fits the Pro 575 main grate without blocking airflow. The grate itself is larger, but leaving a few inches of clearance around the stone is important for convection. The [Unicook Pizza Stone](product:unicook-pizza-stone-heavy-duty-cordierite) and both Hans Grill options hit that dimension and are confirmed fits for the Pro 575 grate.
### How long does it take to preheat a pizza stone on a Traeger?

Budget at least 45 minutes at your target temperature , typically 450, 500°F for a Traeger. The stone's surface may read hot sooner, but the core needs time to saturate with heat. A stone that hasn't fully preheated will produce a pale, undercooked bottom crust even if the top looks done. Use an infrared thermometer to verify surface temperature before launching your first pizza.
### Should I use parchment paper with a pizza stone on a Traeger?

Parchment is useful for the first few launches until you get comfortable with the peel technique, but it limits crisping on the bottom crust. Parchment holds moisture between the dough and the stone surface, which partially defeats the purpose of using a stone. Once you're confident with a peel, launch directly onto the stone. If you use parchment, remove it partway through the cook when the dough is firm enough to hold its shape.
### What is the difference between the standard Hans Grill stone and the PRO XL version?

The stone dimensions are identical at 15 × 12 inches on both. The difference is the included peel: the standard [Hans Grill Pizza Stone](product:hans-grill-pizza-stone-rectangular) ships with a wooden peel, while the [Hans Grill PRO XL](product:hans-grill-pizza-stone-pro-xl-baking) includes a long-handled anodized aluminum peel. For Traeger use, where the grill is hot and deep, the longer aluminum handle is a practical safety upgrade. If you already own a long-handled peel, the two products are functionally equivalent.
