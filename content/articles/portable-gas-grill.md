Grilling away from home doesn't require compromise , not if you choose the right equipment. A well-matched [portable gas grill](/gas-grills/) gets you from cold to cooking in minutes, runs cleanly on a standard propane cylinder, and folds down flat enough to live in a truck bed or a closet until the next trip. The question isn't whether portable gas can deliver real results. The question is which grill fits the way you actually cook.

The differences between models come down to surface area, BTU output, how they handle wind and uneven terrain, and whether they collapse into something you'll actually carry. I've looked at all four options below with those factors in mind.

## What to Look For in a Portable Gas Grill

### Cooking Surface and BTU Output

Surface area is the limiting factor on every portable grill, so it's worth being honest about it before you buy. A grill with 150 square inches can cook four burgers comfortably. A grill with 250 to 300 square inches can handle a mixed load , chicken thighs plus vegetables, or a full rack of ribs cut in half. Know your group size before you set a budget.

BTU output matters less than people think, but it's not irrelevant. You want enough heat to sear properly , generally above 10,000 BTU for a grill this size. What you don't want is a high-BTU rating paired with thin grates that don't retain heat. Grate material matters more than the number on the spec sheet.

### Ignition System

Push-button piezo ignition is the standard on most portable gas grills, and for good reason. It's reliable when the mechanism is well-sealed, doesn't require a lighter or matches, and works in most weather conditions. Where it fails is when the igniter gets wet or the electrode corrodes , both of which happen faster on a grill that travels frequently.

The practical test is whether you can re-light a burner with a long match or barbecue lighter if the ignition fails mid-cook. Any grill without an accessible burner port is a liability in the field. Check the manual before you're standing in a parking lot with raw chicken.

### Stability and Setup

Tabletop grills rest on a folding-leg design that ranges from genuinely stable to actively dangerous on anything but a perfectly flat surface. Before buying, consider where you'll actually use it , tailgate asphalt, a campground picnic table, uneven ground at a park. Foldable legs are convenient, but they need a locking mechanism to hold position under the lateral force of stirring or flipping.

Stand-mounted designs add height and often feel more secure because the footprint is wider. They take a few more seconds to set up, but they eliminate the "grill slide" problem that tabletop designs are prone to on textured surfaces.

### Wind Resistance

Wind is the variable most buyers ignore and most manufacturers understate. Even a moderate breeze can drop burner temperature enough to extend cook times by a third. Look for a lid that closes with a secure latch, side panels or windscreens if the design includes them, and a burner tube orientation that runs parallel rather than perpendicular to likely wind direction.

Convective heating designs manage this better than open-flame burners because the heat is distributed more evenly across the cooking surface rather than concentrated at one point. That matters most on beach and waterfront sites where wind direction is unpredictable.

### Portability and Storage

The weight-to-usefulness ratio is the real trade-off with portable grills. A unit that weighs eighteen pounds and breaks down to twelve inches is genuinely portable. One that weighs eighteen pounds and breaks down to twenty-four inches will probably stay in the truck. Measure your storage space , the back of a Subaru Outback and the bed of a full-size truck are different planning problems.

Look at how the grill latches closed for transport. A locking lid keeps grease off everything else in the load. Side tables and accessories that detach cleanly are worth more than ones that fold in place but catch on everything. Reviewing the full spectrum of [gas grills](/gas-grills/) before buying portable is worth the time , you may realize a compact cart grill solves your actual problem better than a tabletop unit.

## Buying Guide

### Matching the Grill to the Occasion

A tailgate and a backcountry camping trip require different tools, even if both technically justify the word "portable." Tailgating tolerates more weight and size because the grill rides in a vehicle and stays in one place once set up. Backcountry use demands the lightest, most compact option you can cook on responsibly. Figure out which scenario describes 80 percent of your actual use before you choose.

If you split your use between both, the Cuisinart Petite Gourmet is the better choice for weight-sensitive trips. The Royal Gourmet GT2006 handles the tailgate context with more surface area and output.

### Single-Burner vs. Multi-Burner Output

Every grill in this group uses a single burner. That limits temperature zoning , you can't run a hot side and a cool side simultaneously the way you can on a two-burner unit. The practical workaround is sequencing: sear first, then move food to the edges of the grate to finish at lower radiant heat with the lid down.

For most portable cooking scenarios, a single burner is sufficient. You're not usually feeding twelve people at a campsite. Where it becomes a constraint is when you're trying to cook proteins and vegetables simultaneously to different doneness levels. If that's a regular need, consider whether a [gas grill](/gas-grills/) with two burners , even a compact cart model , solves the problem more cleanly.

### BTU Output and Real-World Heat

BTU ratings describe how much energy the burner produces, not how efficiently that energy translates to cooking heat. A higher BTU number in a poorly insulated grill produces worse results than a moderate BTU number in a well-sealed unit. The lid fit, the grate mass, and the windscreen design all affect how much of that burner output reaches your food.

Use BTU as a floor, not a ceiling. Below 10,000 BTU on a portable grill, you'll struggle to sear effectively at altitude or in cold weather. Above that threshold, the other design factors matter more than the number.

### Propane Compatibility and Cylinder Size

All four grills in this group run on standard 1-pound propane canisters , the green Coleman-style cylinders available at any hardware store or campground supply shop. Most are also adaptable to a standard 20-pound tank via an aftermarket hose, which is worth knowing if you plan to use the grill at a fixed tailgate setup rather than hauling it into the field.

One-pound cylinders run out faster than most people expect. A 12,000 BTU burner at full heat will exhaust a 1-pound cylinder in roughly 45 to 60 minutes of continuous use. Budget two cylinders per session if you're cooking for more than two people.

### Cleaning and Long-Term Maintenance

Portable grills get cleaned less consistently than backyard grills, and that neglect accelerates deterioration. Grease that sits in a folded, closed grill between trips carbonizes onto the grates and migrates into the burner ports. A fifteen-minute cleaning after every session extends the usable life of any of these units significantly.

Cast iron grates require seasoning and are not ideal for a grill that will sit unused for weeks at a time. Porcelain-coated or stainless grates are lower maintenance for intermittent-use portable grills. Check the grate material before buying if your cooking schedule is irregular.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Which portable gas grill is best for solo camping trips?

The [Cuisinart Petite Gourmet](product:cuisinart-petite-gourmet-portable-gas) is the right answer for solo use. Its compact footprint, low weight, and VersaStand adjustability make it the easiest to carry and set up on unpredictable terrain. The 150 square inch cooking surface handles one to two portions without issue, and the 5,500 BTU output is adequate for the moderate cooking loads a solo camper typically manages.
### How long will a 1-pound propane cylinder last on these grills?

At full burner output, a 1-pound cylinder lasts roughly 45 to 60 minutes of continuous cooking. In practice, most cooks reduce heat after initial preheating, which stretches cylinder life. Bring two cylinders for any session longer than an hour or any gathering larger than two people , running out mid-cook is a predictable problem with a simple solution.
### Is the Charbroil convective system noticeably different from a standard gas grill?

Yes, in a way that matters in specific conditions. The convective design distributes heat more evenly and handles wind better than an open-flame burner. You won't get the same aggressive sear marks, but you'll get more consistent results across the cooking surface without the hot-spot management that standard single-burner designs require. For coastal or open-field cooking, that trade-off favors the Charbroil.
### Can I use these grills on a wooden picnic table?

The grills themselves don't generate excessive external heat, but foldable-leg tabletop designs should always be placed on a heat-resistant surface or an elevated grill mat when used on wood. The underside of the grill body does get warm during extended use. A silicone grill mat rated for outdoor use is an inexpensive precaution worth carrying alongside any portable tabletop grill.
### Does the Megamaster's locking lid actually matter for transport?

It matters more than buyers expect. A locking lid keeps the grill closed when it shifts in a truck bed or gets jostled in cargo. Grease inside a portable grill doesn't stay contained without a positive latch , and a grill that opens in transit transfers that mess to everything around it. If you're loading a grill into a vehicle frequently, the locking mechanism on the [Megamaster](product:megamaster-1-burner-portable-gas) is a genuine functional advantage over designs that rely on a friction fit or no closure at all.
