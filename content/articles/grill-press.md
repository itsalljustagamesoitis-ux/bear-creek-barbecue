A grill press is one of those tools that looks simple but separates the cooks who get a proper sear from those who get a steam bath in a pan. Whether you're smashing burgers on a flat-top or pressing bacon flat on a cast iron grate, the press you choose determines how much contact, and therefore how much crust, you actually get. If you're still shopping around the broader [tools and grates](/tools-and-grates/) category, it's worth understanding what separates a useful press from a frustrating one before you buy.

The market is almost entirely cast iron, which is the right call , but not all cast iron presses are built the same. Handle design, size, and bottom geometry matter more than most listings let on.

## What to Look For in a Grill Press

### Bottom Surface and Edge Design

The bottom of the press is where all the work happens. A flat, smooth bottom transfers heat evenly and creates full contact across the meat's surface. Some presses have ridged or textured bottoms, which reduce contact area and undercut the whole point of pressing.

Edge geometry matters for smash burgers specifically. A press with a flat, flush edge allows you to press a ball of beef all the way to the perimeter, creating that thin, lacy crust around the outside. A press with a raised lip or curved edge stops short and leaves the outer rim under-pressed. If crispy edges are the goal , and they should be , look for a flat-bottomed press with an edge that reaches the surface cleanly.

### Handle Design and Heat Management

The handle is where most cheap presses fail. Cast iron retains heat aggressively, and a press sitting on a 450-degree griddle will conduct that heat straight up to whatever you're gripping. A wooden handle won't transmit heat the way bare metal will, and a spiral or coil design adds air gap insulation.

The grip itself matters for extended cooking sessions. A short, stubby handle gives you less mechanical leverage, meaning you have to push harder to achieve the same pressure. Look for a handle long enough to keep your hand clear of the cooking surface, with enough grip area to apply force without fatigue.

### Weight and Size

A heavier press applies more passive pressure, which matters if you're pressing something flat and walking away for a moment rather than actively pushing down. A lighter press requires more active effort to maintain contact. Neither is strictly better , it depends on how you cook , but knowing the trade-off helps you choose.

Size is more straightforward. A press that's too small for a burger patty means you're pressing in sections, which creates uneven results. A press that's too large for your grill grate becomes unwieldy. Most griddle setups handle a 6.5-to-8-inch round press without issue. For grill grates with bars, a smaller rectangular press can navigate between the bars more cleanly than a wide round. Browse the full range of [grill tools and grates](/tools-and-grates/) if you're outfitting more than just a press , it pays to think about what else will share your cooking surface.

### Seasoning and Maintenance

Every cast iron press needs seasoning , either factory-applied or your own , before it touches food. A factory-seasoned press gives you a head start, but that initial seasoning is rarely sufficient on its own. After the first few uses, the press will start building up a more functional non-stick layer through normal cooking.

The maintenance ask is real. Rust is the enemy of bare cast iron, and a press that sits wet will deteriorate quickly. Dry it immediately after washing, apply a thin layer of oil, and store it somewhere with low humidity. It's not complicated, but it's not optional either.

## Buying Guide

### Round vs. Rectangular , Which Shape Fits Your Setup

Shape is the first decision because it's determined almost entirely by your cooking surface, not your preference. A round press is purpose-built for flat-top griddles where it can rotate freely and press round patties from center to edge. A rectangular press navigates grill grates more cleanly , it sits across the bars rather than rocking on them.

If you cook exclusively on a griddle, round is the natural choice. If you cook on both surfaces, the Firsgrill kit's dual-shape approach is worth considering. Don't buy a round press and expect it to work well on a traditional grill grate without frustration.

### Handle Type and How Long You're Cooking

A short metal handle works fine for a quick press and release. It becomes a problem over a longer session where the handle absorbs enough heat to make gripping uncomfortable. For anything beyond casual use, prioritize a wood handle or a coil/spiral design that insulates by geometry.

Consider how you'll use the press in practice. If you're making four burgers on a Tuesday night, handle heat is a minor concern. If you're cooking for fifteen people and pressing constantly for forty-five minutes, a handle that stays manageable matters. The Lodge spiral handle is the most engineered solution here; wood handles are a close second for longer sessions.

### Size Relative to Your Patties and Your Surface

Match the press to the size of what you're pressing. A press that's too small for your patty means incomplete coverage, uneven crust, and patchy results. A press that's too large for your cooking surface is awkward and risks crowding adjacent food.

For standard smash burgers , starting with a two-ounce ball of beef , a 6.5-to-7-inch press gives you full coverage as it spreads. For larger patties or whole chicken pieces, the 8-inch Lodge round covers more ground. Checking the dimensions of your specific [grill tools and grates](/tools-and-grates/) before buying prevents size mismatches that are frustrating in practice.

### Cast Iron Maintenance , The Honest Assessment

Every press on this list requires maintenance. That's not a flaw , it's the nature of cast iron , but it's worth being clear-eyed about what you're committing to. After each use: wash with hot water, dry immediately and thoroughly, apply a thin layer of neutral oil, and store dry. Do this consistently and a cast iron press will outlast everything else in your kit. Skip it and you'll find rust within a few weeks.

Pre-seasoned presses reduce the effort required on first use but don't eliminate maintenance going forward. The Lodge presses carry a particularly solid factory seasoning, but they still need the same post-use routine as everything else.

### Who Should Skip a Grill Press Entirely

A grill press is not useful for everything. Thick steaks benefit from direct contact with a hot surface, not pressure , pressing a ribeye forces out juices without improving the crust. The press earns its place with smash burgers, bacon, panini, and thin cuts where maximizing contact area is the goal.

If your cooking skews toward thick cuts and traditional grilling, a press will spend most of its time on a shelf. If your setup includes a griddle and you make smash burgers regularly, it becomes one of the tools you reach for automatically.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What's the difference between a burger press and a grill press?

A burger press typically refers to a mold used to shape raw patties before cooking. A grill press is a weighted tool pressed down on food while it's cooking, used to maximize surface contact and improve searing. The terms are sometimes used interchangeably in listings, which creates confusion , look at the product description to confirm whether it's a pre-cook shaping tool or an in-cook pressing tool.
### Do I need a grill press if I already cook on a flat-top griddle?

A flat-top griddle already provides a full-contact cooking surface, so a grill press is most useful there for smash burgers, where you're actively pressing a ball of beef into a thin patty. For standard patties you've pre-shaped, the griddle surface alone does most of the work. The press becomes essential when the technique calls for it, not as a universal requirement.
### How do I keep a cast iron grill press from rusting?
Dry it completely after every wash , this is the step most people skip. After drying with a towel, put it on a warm burner for a minute to drive off any remaining moisture. While it's still warm, apply a thin coat of neutral oil , flaxseed, vegetable, or canola , and wipe off the excess. Store it somewhere dry.
### Is the Lodge rectangular press or the Lodge round press better for a traditional grill?

The rectangular [Lodge Pre-Seasoned Cast Iron Grill Press](product:lodge-pre-seasoned-cast-iron) is the better fit for a traditional grill with bars. Its shape sits across the grates without rocking, and its smaller footprint makes it easier to maneuver between items. The 8-inch round press is better suited to a flat griddle surface where its full diameter can make contact cleanly.
### Can I use a grill press on a pellet grill like a Traeger?

Yes, but with the understanding that pellet grills don't produce the same high-direct-heat surface as a gas burner or charcoal grill. The press works best when the surface underneath is genuinely hot , at least 400 degrees. On a pellet grill with a griddle insert running at full temperature, a grill press performs well. On the standard grill grates at normal cooking temperatures, the press adds limited value because the surface contact advantage requires intense direct heat to create a crust.
